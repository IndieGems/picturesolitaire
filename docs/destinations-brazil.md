# Brazil: candidate destinations

Trip 17, boards 241–255 (creator, 2026-11-08: a trip for each language the game speaks that had
none, "except russia"). Trips 17–22 ship together in one build. Like trips 11–16, they add no new
art except their postcards and stamps: every everyday picture comes from the library. Each board
has one real place, shown as a postcard the size of the whole board. A 2×2 part of it, the
**crop**, plays on the board. How postcards are made is in `docs/picture-direction.md`
("Destinations").

**Shapes** (Levels, 2026-11-08):
- Most boards are 4×5, with a tall 3:5 postcard.
- 250, the hard board, is 5×5, with a 3:4 postcard.
- The finale, 255, is 6×6, with a 3:4 postcard.
- 246 is the all-long board: a 4×4 postcard whose window is a two-card strip, here a wide one.

**Tags.** *famous* is a place everyone knows. *hidden gem* is a place few players will know
but they'll want to visit. *silly* is something funny or odd. The mix is 8 famous, 4 gems and
3 silly. The trip lands in Rio, goes south to Paraná and Santa Catarina, west to Bonito and the
Pantanal, up the north-east coast and then up the Amazon to Manaus. It visits 11 states, and
no state has more than two boards.

**The rights rules, first** (checked 2026-11-08).
- **Brazil's freedom of panorama is wide.** Article 48 of the copyright law (Lei 9.610/1998)
  lets anyone represent a work that stands permanently in a public place "by paintings,
  drawings, photographs and audiovisual processes". That covers buildings and outdoor art
  alike. All the same, modern art and architecture stay out by our own rule: Niemeyer's
  Brasília, Pampulha and Niterói, Lina Bo Bardi's MASP, Burle Marx's Copacabana promenade and
  the Selarón steps.
- **Licensed images.**
  - Christ the Redeemer is out. It is a sacred statue, and the archdiocese licenses its image.
    It must not appear even small on its mountain, so the Rio postcard faces away from Corcovado.
  - **Sugarloaf needs the lead's ruling (hold).** The cable-car operator, Parque Bondinho Pão
    de Açúcar (a registered brand), claims the "commercial image" of the hills, the cables and
    the cable car, and in 2023 it objected to a published photo of its cable car. Lawyers
    writing in Conjur answered that the landscape belongs to the public: the operator made no
    work there. The mountain is a public natural monument, and article 48 lets anyone paint it.
    The postcard paints the mountain from Botafogo beach, with no cable car, cables, stations or
    brand. If the lead says no, Ouro Preto's streets take board 241.
  - Football grounds, Carnival's samba schools and Havaianas are brands: none appears.
- **Permits.** National parks need an ICMBio permit to film or photograph for commercial use
  on site, and indigenous lands need FUNAI's. We do neither: every postcard is painted from
  words. Places that can't be visited at all are out: Snake Island (closed by the Navy) and
  Ilha Fiscal (a Navy site). So are Bonito's tour farms by name. Brazil's rivers are public,
  so the postcard names the rivers, not a farm.
- **No sacred places, tombs or tragedies.**
  - Churches are out: Ouro Preto's, Olinda's, Salvador's (São Francisco, Bonfim), Aparecida and
    the Jesuit ruins of São Miguel das Missões.
  - Monte Roraima is sacred to the Pemon, and it is on the Venezuela and Guyana border.
  - Indigenous villages and their ceremonies are out.
  - So are the sites of the Mariana and Brumadinho dam disasters, and Capitólio's canyon, where
    a cliff fell on tourists in 2022.
- **No people.** That rules out favelas (including Vidigal on the Dois Irmãos peaks above
  Ipanema), Carnival, beach crowds, Olinda's giant puppets and Bahia's baianas. Paraty's
  streets, the beached rafts and the markets are empty, at dawn.
- **No flags, signs or lettering.** Brazil's flag is not painted anywhere. The Theatro
  Amazonas dome's tiles are green, blue and gold, but as tiles, never as a flag. The Lacerda
  Elevator and the markets have no signs.
- **No borders.** Iguaçu is out (it is Argentina's postcard, trip 7) and so is Itaipu (a
  company's dam, on the Paraguay border).

**Library look-alikes.** These postcards take the family of an everyday picture like them,
so the two never share a board:
- Paraty at high tide takes *The Flood* (flooded-village).
- The Hercílio Luz Bridge takes *Golden Gate*.
- The Pantanal jaguar takes *Ocelot*.
- The hyacinth macaws take *Scarlet Macaw*.
- The sloth takes *Sloth*. The cocoa pods stay small, because *Cocoa Pod* is in the library too.
- The jangadas take *Dhow*, the other triangular sail on a wooden hull.
- The pink river dolphin takes *Dolphin*.

The other eight are like no library picture and get families of their own: sugarloaf,
goblet-rock, clear-river, lacerda, cashew, white-dunes, iron-market and theatro.

**Shipped postcards, not repeated.**
- Sugarloaf is a rounded granite dome over a city bay, so it is not like Kirkjufell's pointed
  peak or Table Mountain's flat top.
- **Lençóis Maranhenses must not look like Whitehaven** (a white-and-turquoise aerial swirl) or
  the Dune du Pilat (a golden ridge over the sea). It is painted from the ground: a clear
  green-blue lagoon in front and round white dunes behind.
- The Cup of Vila Velha is a single sandstone goblet standing in grassland. Bryce's hoodoos,
  the Monkey Fingers and the Pinnacles are fields of spires, and the Remarkable Rocks are a
  hollow boulder on the sea.
- Paraty is white houses with coloured doors, on flooded cobbles. It is not a pastel row: six
  postcards already are (Burano, Caminito, Brighton, Bo-Kaap, Costa Nova, Rainbow Row). Its doors
  must not look like Dublin's red, yellow and blue doors in a brick terrace.
- The jangadas are white triangular sails on a beach under red cliffs. Essaouira's blue boats
  have no sails.
- **Left out as look-alikes:**
  - Morro Dois Irmãos at Fernando de Noronha: nine postcards are already sea stacks.
  - The Lapa Arches: five are viaducts or aqueducts.
  - Pelourinho: it would be the seventh pastel row.
  - The Fumaça and Itaimbezinho falls: six are waterfalls.
  - Bonito's Blue Lake Cave, like Phong Nha and Benagil.
  - Serra do Rio do Rastro, like Sani Pass.
  - Chapada dos Veadeiros' Moon Valley, like Bourke's Luck Potholes.
  - Morro do Pai Inácio, like Table Mountain.
  - Jericoacoara's Pedra Furada, like Durdle Door.
  - Jericoacoara's Lazy Tree, like Wanaka's tree.
  - Capybaras (Iberá, trip 7), caimans (the Everglades alligator) and the jabiru (El Badi's
    storks).
- **Left out for the next trip:** Gramado, Blumenau, Pomerode and Holambra are German and Dutch
  towns in Brazil, and would look like trip 18 or like Consuegra's windmills.

## Left out, and why

- **Christ the Redeemer:** sacred, and its image is licensed.
- **Iguaçu and Itaipu:** Argentina's postcard, and a company's dam on a border.
- **The Selarón steps, Copacabana's promenade, Brasília, Pampulha, Niterói and MASP:** modern
  art and architecture.
- **Favelas, Carnival, Olinda's giant puppets and beach crowds:** people.
- **Churches and the Missões ruins:** sacred.
- **Monte Roraima:** sacred to the Pemon, on a border.
- **Snake Island and Ilha Fiscal:** closed, or the Navy's.
- **Maracanã and football:** brands.
- **Mariana, Brumadinho and Capitólio:** disasters.
- **Gramado, Blumenau, Pomerode and Holambra:** they would look like the next trip.
- **Look-alikes of shipped postcards:** Noronha's peaks, the Lapa Arches, Pelourinho, the
  waterfalls, Bonito's cave, Serra do Rio do Rastro, Moon Valley, Pai Inácio, Jericoacoara,
  capybaras, caimans and the jabiru.

That is 4 oil paintings, 3 watercolours, 3 gouaches, 3 naïf paintings and 2 travel posters.
Each style shares the place's culture and period, and none copies a real picture:
- Sugarloaf's watercolour follows the travelling artists of the 1810s–20s (Thomas Ender,
  Rugendas, Debret), who painted Rio's bay.
- The macaws' watercolour follows Hercule Florence's watercolours of Mato Grosso's animals from
  the Langsdorff expedition (1820s).
- Ver-o-Peso's watercolour follows the rubber-boom views of Belém around 1900.
- The naïf paintings follow Brazil's own *arte naïf*: Paraty's galleries are full of it, and so
  is Bahia's tradition.
- The Cup's oil follows Alfredo Andersen's landscapes of Paraná.
- The jaguar's oil follows 19th-century naturalist painting.
- The jangadas' oil follows the Brazilian coastal painters of the 1940s.
- The Theatro's oil follows the academic painting of its own 1890s.
- The posters follow 1920s–30s travel posters, the decades of the bridge (1926) and the
  elevator's Art Deco tower (1930).
- The dolphin's gouache follows Margaret Mee's Amazon gouaches, painted on the Rio Negro.
  Bonito and Lençóis are mid-century gouache illustration.

The naïf postcards get a new style line (*postcard-naif*), alongside the American folk one.

**Stamp t17:** a toco toucan on a branch in a ring of tropical leaves, in a sunflower-yellow ink
(hue about 52). It sits between Australia's golden ochre (38) and Italy's olive (76), so it is
painted paler and purer than the ochre and checked beside all 16 stamps.

**Cost:** 15 postcards at medium quality are about $0.24, and the stamp about $0.05. With a
few redraws the trip comes to about $0.40.
